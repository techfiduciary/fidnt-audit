The commitment-fee trap: what the House did to foreign-assisted projects, and where the law leaves the door open

HB 10858 vs the 2027 NEP — a first-pass assessment built on the coalition's DPWH findings, extended to the financing agreements underneath the cuts.

FIDNT audit node · for BetterGov × People's Budget Coalition vetting · as of 11 Oct 2026 · first pass, not a conclusion

§1Key takeaways

  • The House did not shrink DPWH — it swapped its portfolio. Additions of ₱87.2B in local projects nearly cancel ₱73.0B removed from foreign-assisted lines, netting only ₱11.5B. The headline number hides the trade.
  • 6,033 projects in the House bill are not in the NEP (₱165.6B), concentrated in access roads and multi-purpose buildings; 1,160 NEP projects (₱68.4B) were dropped.
  • 17 lines (₱3.1B) double-pay projects the same bill already funds — same bridge, second line. Duplications are real and countable.
  • A cut to a foreign-assisted project is not a saving — it is a liability. Principal and interest remain automatically appropriated; commitment fees keep accruing on every undisbursed peso; and without a GAA line item the loan proceeds cannot be released even if they sit in the Treasury. We pay to not build.
  • The release blockage is administrative, not statutory. ODA loan proceeds are managed as trust receipts; conditioning their release on a GAA line item is DBM practice. That is fixable by circular — and challengeable where it is not fixed.

§3Why a cut is not a saving: the money flow

Foreign-assisted projects sit on signed loan agreements. The obligations in those agreements do not disappear when a line item does. Here is the flow that turns a congressional "cut" into a taxpayer liability:

1 · SignedLoan agreement signed; commitment fee begins accruing on the undisbursed balance once the availability period opens.
2 · NEP lineThe project enters the NEP with a line item; ICC/NEDA Board cleared it at appraisal.
3 · House cutHB 10858 deletes the line item (₱73.0B across FAPs by the first pass).
4 · BlockedNo GAA line → no release to the implementing agency, even though the proceeds are already in the Treasury as trust receipts.
5 · Still owedPrincipal + interest remain automatic appropriations; commitment fees keep accruing on the undisbursed balance. We pay to not build.
The asymmetry in one line: debt service is automatic; the project funding is not. Congress can delete the line item, but it cannot delete the loan. The gap between the two is paid by the Filipino taxpayer — in interest, in commitment fees, and in the foregone project.

§4The financing-agreement brief: where the fight can go better

The DOF's financing-agreements library and the ODA framework have more room in them than the "we can't do anything, the loan is signed" reflex suggests. Seven openings, ordered by leverage:

1 · The release blockage is a circular, not a statute — so it can be fixed by circular

ODA loan proceeds are accounted for as trust receipts, not general funds. Conditioning their release to implementing agencies on a GAA line item is DBM budgeting practice, carried in issuances — not an act of Congress. The statute (the ODA Act framework and the Administrative Code's fund-release rules) does not command the blockage.

The push: a NEDA–DBM–DOF joint circular delinking trust-receipt release from GAA line items for signed, ICC-cleared projects — release against the financing agreement's disbursement conditions, with COA audit intact. Demand it in the budget season, not after.

2 · Nobody prices the cut: commitment-fee disclosure as a standing rule

When the House deletes a foreign-assisted line, the printed bill shows a "saving." Nothing in the document shows the commitment fee that keeps accruing on the undisbursed balance, the FX exposure, or the rescheduling cost. The BESF (Budget of Expenditures and Sources of Financing) does not carry a per-project commitment-fee exposure table.

The push: a budget-document reform — every FAP line deleted or reduced must print its remaining loan balance, availability period, commitment-fee rate, and accrued fees. Make the hidden cost a printed number before the vote.

3 · Zombie loans: demand cancellation, not slow bleed

A line-itemed-out project does not simply stop. The loan stays signed, the availability period runs, fees accrue — a zombie. The ODA framework provides for renegotiation and termination of financing arrangements; a loan that Congress has defunded is the textbook case for a formal restructuring or cancellation conversation with the creditor, which stops the fee clock.

The push: a standing DOF report to Congress listing every defunded FAP with its loan status and a recommended action — cancel, restructure, or re-fund. Silence is the expensive option.

4 · The immunity-waiver boundary is already drawn — hold them to it

DOF's own public briefing on the loan agreements states the posture: no collaterals; waiver of sovereign immunity limited to arbitration as a mode of dispute settlement; and arbitral rulings still subject to the Philippine Constitution, courts, and public policy. Anything in a financing agreement that reaches past that boundary — exit denial, outer-space assets, diplomatic-property attachment language of the kind reported in the China-area loans — exceeds the stated policy and is challengeable on the government's own terms.

The push: line-by-line review of the General Terms and Conditions of the newest agreements against DOF's stated posture; flag every clause wider than the briefing.

5 · The ICC cleared it, Congress defunded it — no recoordination duty exists

Every FAP passed through the Investment Coordination Committee and the NEDA Board — now the DEPDev Board under RA 12145 (2025) — before the agreement was signed. When Congress deletes the line, no mechanism forces the appraisal logic back to the table: the country keeps the obligation while abandoning the appraisal's benefit case. The approval architecture is administrative (ICC guidelines under RA 12145), and nothing in it — nor in the ODA Act itself — imposes a duty to recoordinate a signed, cleared project with its financing when the GAA defunds it.

The push: an ODA Act amendment (or NEDA Board policy) requiring a defunded-but-signed project to return to the ICC within one budget cycle — cancel with a fee accounting, or re-fund with reasons. No more zombies by default.

6 · Availability periods expire — the money disappears either way

Undisbursed loan balances cancel when the availability period lapses. A project blocked for years by repeated line-item cuts can simply lose the financing at expiry — after years of commitment fees on money that never moved. The GAA says nothing about which defunded projects are approaching expiry.

The push: availability-period disclosure per FAP in the BESF, with a twelve-month warning list — so defunding decisions are made knowing the clock.

7 · The public loan library is stale — signed terms the public cannot vet

DOF's own financing-agreements library — the public's window into what the country signs — has not posted a new agreement entry since August 2023, even as new program and subscription loans continue to be signed and reported in press releases. In 2019, facing the China-loan controversies, DOF committed to full public disclosure of loan terms. A library two budget cycles behind is not disclosure.

The push: a standing rule — every financing agreement posted to the library within 30 days of signature, with its General Terms and Conditions attached. Congress votes on loan-funded projects every year; it should not vote blind.

§5Questions for the Senate finance committee

  1. For each foreign-assisted project deleted or reduced in HB 10858: what is the remaining loan balance, the availability period, and the commitment fee that accrues per year if the project is not implemented?
  2. What has the Republic paid in commitment fees over the last five years on loans for projects that were delayed or defunded — by creditor, and by project?
  3. Which DBM issuance conditions the release of ODA loan proceeds on a GAA line item — and will DBM, NEDA, and DOF issue a joint circular releasing trust-receipt proceeds for signed, ICC-cleared projects without one?
  4. Which of the defunded projects are within 24 months of availability-period expiry, and what happens to the undisbursed balances then?
  5. Will DOF initiate renegotiation or cancellation talks for projects Congress has defunded, rather than letting commitment fees run — and will it report the outcome per loan?
  6. Do the newest financing agreements' General Terms and Conditions stay within DOF's stated posture — no collateral, immunity waiver limited to arbitration, rulings subject to the Constitution, courts, and public policy? Clause by clause, which do not?
